## Investigating the affective use of ChatGPT

As AI systems become more capable of natural conversation, an important question emerges: how do people use these systems for emotional and affective purposes, and what does that mean for their wellbeing?

This question is particularly important as concerns grow about the potential emotional dependency that might arise from people forming relationships with AI. To understand how these dynamics actually manifest, we need data on real interactions. In this post, we share two complementary studies: an analysis of millions of anonymized ChatGPT conversations, and a large-scale survey of ChatGPT users about how they use the system and how they feel it affects their lives.

Our collaborators in this research include researchers from the MIT Media Lab and other institutions, and the work builds on our earlier research on how people use ChatGPT.

## Key questions

How common is affective use? What fraction of ChatGPT conversations involve emotional or affective content?

Who engages in affective use, and how? What demographic and usage patterns characterize people who interact with ChatGPT affectively?

How do users feel about it? What effects—positive or negative—do users report from emotional interactions with ChatGPT?

## Study 1: What happens in conversations

We analyzed a sample of millions of anonymized conversations from ChatGPT, focusing on conversations where users discussed emotions, personal problems, relationships, or sought emotional support.

Overall prevalence of affective use remains low but is growing. Around 0.15% of all messages in our sample were classified as affective conversations (requests for emotional support, discussions of personal struggles, or relationship-like interactions). While this is a small fraction of overall usage, the absolute number has been growing as usage overall grows.

Most conversations don’t turn personal. The vast majority of ChatGPT conversations remain task-focused—writing, coding, information seeking—and do not drift into personal or emotional territory.

The most common affective topics are everyday life and work stress, followed by relationship and family concerns. Discussions of mental health conditions such as anxiety and depression are less common but present.

## Study 2: What users report

We surveyed thousands of ChatGPT users about their usage patterns and wellbeing. Key findings:

Most users who interact with ChatGPT affectively do so episodically, not habitually. The majority report discussing an emotional topic occasionally, rather than relying on the system as a primary source of support.

Extreme or concerning use is rare. A small minority of users report turning to ChatGPT frequently for emotional support, and this pattern is associated with—though not necessarily caused by—lower scores on standard wellbeing measures.

Loneliness and social connection correlate with usage patterns. Users who report higher loneliness are somewhat more likely to use ChatGPT for emotional conversation, though the direction of causality is unclear.

Users generally report positive or neutral effects. Most users who use ChatGPT affectively report that it helps them think through problems, provides comfort, or serves as a sounding board; a small percentage report feeling worse after use.

## Implications for product design

These findings inform how we approach product decisions:

Design for occasional use, not dependency. We design ChatGPT to be helpful when people need to talk, while not encouraging heavy emotional reliance. This includes break reminders during long sessions and clear messaging that ChatGPT is an AI, not a person.

Care with vulnerable users. When conversations involve potential mental health crises, ChatGPT provides information about professional resources and crisis hotlines.

Continued research. We are expanding our research in this area, including longitudinal studies of how usage patterns evolve over time.

## Caveats and limitations

Our analysis has important limitations:

Anonymized conversations cannot be linked to individual wellbeing outcomes. The conversation analysis shows what happens in chats, not how users feel.

Survey data is self-reported and correlational. We cannot draw causal conclusions about whether ChatGPT use affects wellbeing.

Automated classification of conversations is imperfect, and some affective conversations may be misclassified.

We are committed to continuing to study these questions rigorously and sharing what we learn.
