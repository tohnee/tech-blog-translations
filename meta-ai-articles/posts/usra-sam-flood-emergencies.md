December 18, 2025 • 9 minute read The Universities Space Research Association (USRA) and Meta are collaborating to help support water observing systems set up by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) . These systems play a crucial role in forecasting floods and droughts, managing reservoirs for water supply and power generation, and guiding water management decisions that protect the safety and wellbeing of communities across the United States. By integrating AI models like SAM into operational systems, emergency operators can enhance their ability to respond swiftly to evolving conditions and safeguard residents. In 2019, Congress provided funding to USGS to begin piloting a Next Generation Water Observing System (NGWOS) . Since then, NGWOS has operated in several medium-sized basins, deploying new monitoring stations, sensors, and technologies that fill critical information gaps for water preservation and disaster management. To support this effort, USGS teamed up with USRA’s Research Institute for Advanced Computer Science (RIACS) to explore how artificial intelligence can complement traditional water analysis. Through this partnership, USRA data scientists collaborate closely with USGS experts to identify opportunities where AI can add value. One important project uses drone imagery to map river flows in approximate real time. This approach can be rapidly deployed to assist in areas of interest. A second project is developing approaches for improved monitoring of river extent from satellite imagery with a case study of the Chesapeake Bay area. For this collaboration, USRA leveraged Meta’s Segment Anything Model (SAM), a foundational model for image segmentation that lets people segment objects in images and videos with minimal training. To support identification of water in drone and satellite imagery, USRA research scientists developed a fine-tuned version of SAM 2, which amplified the ability of the model to recognize water through targeted adaptation. Fine-tuned versions of SAM, trained with USGS and other datasets, can identify streams and detect water extent at scale when combined with remote sensing data from drones and satellites including high-resolution PlanetScope satellite imagery (available through NASA’s Commercial Satellite Data Acquisition Program) and data from the USGS 3D Hydrography Program. This method has delivered promising results in case studies and shows potential for nationwide implementation. Beyond basic water monitoring, this system offers potential benefits for disaster preparedness and response and cost-effective resource management. When applied to real-time workflows, the automated approach detects changes in water conditions that could indicate impending floods, while also providing significant cost savings over traditional monitoring methods. “Meta’s Segment Anything Model (SAM) was used to automate a key part of the overall workflow that our USGS colleagues have described as the main bottleneck for real-time image analysis that previously was done with a labor-intensive manual approach to digitize water boundaries in each image, which is critical for rapid response use cases and scaling solutions nationally and beyond,” says Dr. David Bell, USRA Director. From 1980 until 2024, more than 400 weather-related disasters cost more than $1 billion (CPI adjusted to 2024). These disasters resulted in nearly $3 trillion in damages/costs . Of that number, more than $2 trillion is estimated to be from water-related disasters, such as flooding, cyclones, and drought — with $200 billion of that the result of severe flooding. Furthermore, the five-year average for costs/damages has more than tripled in the last 20 years, according to the data. As the frequency and impact of natural disasters continues to rise, it’s essential to refine and adapt tools like SAM to meet emerging challenges and support proactive mitigation efforts. With the recent release of SAM 3 , USRA and USGS will be able to advance their research through unified detection, segmentation, and tracking of objects in images and video using text, exemplar, and visual prompts. Accurate real-time monitoring and forecasting are important tools for protecting communities from disasters, especially in high-risk areas across the country. The power of public-private partnerships — combining open source AI innovation, research expertise, and federal agency knowledge — demonstrates how artificial intelligence can drive meaningful progress in disaster preparedness and resilience.
